Using several lamps

Choose how many lamps to add, then select a numbered lamp to set its recording, brightness, flicker strength and position. Each lamp keeps its own playback time. Play all recordings starts the active lamps from their current times. Restart all together returns them to the start of their recordings. To compare aligned flicker, choose the same recording and Recorded speed for each lamp before restarting.

Where their light overlaps, the lamps add illumination. A second lamp can fill a shadow cast by the first. When their flicker rises and falls at different times, the combined light can vary less than either source on its own. Wall shadows can be switched off to compare their contribution. Lascaux and the age of the paintings

Lascaux was discovered in 1940. The Whole cave view opens in the Axial Gallery, with other locations available from the cave menu. Radiocarbon dates on reindeer bones place some human activity in the cave around 21,500–21,000 calendar years ago. That gives an approximate setting for the art. The date of each painting remains open. Cave layout · Radiocarbon study.

Shaft Scene

A bird-headed figure lies between a rhinoceros and a bison. The bison lowers its head and its entrails are shown below its belly. Follow the lines from the figure’s outstretched arms to the bison and the bird on a stick. A reindeer bone found beside the sandstone lamp dates to roughly 21,900–21,300 calendar years ago. Hall of the Bulls

Four large aurochs arc across a wall covered with pale calcite. Among their outlines are smaller horses and deer. About the lighting

The default is the first 120 seconds of the lamp recording shown in the PaleoFlicker poster. It plays at its original speed, with an estimated dominant rhythm of 8.8 Hz, or nearly nine cycles of flicker per second. Recorded speed restores the original timing after you adjust the slider.

The lamp follows the signal from a photodiode, a sensor that turns changes in light into changes in voltage. The viewer filters out the fastest fluctuations before playback. The readout shows how far the voltage is above or below the recording’s mean. Quieter recordings receive more display gain so their flicker stays visible. The wall colours come from survey photographs, and the light levels are illustrative.

Flicker strength adjusts how far the light varies around the selected brightness. Set it to 0% for steady light while the recording continues. The viewer limits the largest changes in wall brightness.

The lamp model is a reconstruction based on the museum’s record of the original. The same recorded signal drives the flame animation and the light on the walls. The flame’s shape and size are illustrative.

The PaleoFlicker study

Could the rhythms of a flame shape the rhythms of the brain? The PaleoFlicker pilot measured light and recorded electrical activity from the scalp (EEG) while people watched a replica Lascaux lamp or an open fire. The two minutes of lamplight replayed above contain a sustained flicker at about 8–9 cycles per second. The frequencies in the example fire recording shifted over time.

In all four lamp viewers, the strongest relationship between light and EEG, compared with recordings paired across different times, fell between 8.75 and 9.75 Hz. That concentration weakened when the comparison more closely matched the strength and regularity of the flicker. In the fire recordings, peaks occurred across several frequency ranges.

Whether flicker from a flame can shift an ongoing brain rhythm, or change what someone sees on a painted wall, remains open.
